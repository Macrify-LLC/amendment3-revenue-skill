# The program screen

**Purpose.** After Amendment 3, a county or city may spend its property tax money only on the uses listed in the constitution (Art. VII, § 9(a)(2), see `amendment3.md` §2). This step goes through the government's programs one by one with the user and sorts them:

1. Programs that clearly fit a listed use: keep paying for them with property tax.
2. Programs that **seem** not to fit: ask the user whether they agree. If they do, work out with them what to do about it.

Those decisions drive everything after this step. A program that stays on property tax needs no new charge. A program that moves off property tax needs a fee, an assessment, another source, a redesign, or a decision to stop.

**Who decides.** Whether a program fits a category is a legal judgment. The skill only says when a program *seems* to fall outside the list, and why. The user decides. Never tell the user a program is barred.

**Language.** Talk to the user as if they are not a lawyer or a budget professional. Short sentences. Everyday words. Explain any term the first time you use it (for example, "a special assessment is a charge on a property for a service that benefits that property, billed with the property tax but not covered by the homestead exemption"). Put citations in parentheses or a footnote, never in the middle of the explanation.

---

## Step 1: Explain the list in plain words

Show this table, then ask whether the user's attorney has already reached a view on how broadly to read it.

| What the constitution says property tax may pay for | In plain words |
|---|---|
| Public safety, including law enforcement, fire service and emergency medical service | Police, fire, ambulance/EMS |
| Education and public schools | Education. Whether a library or educational program counts is a question for your attorney |
| Infrastructure, including roads, bridges and stormwater control | Building and maintaining roads, bridges, drainage and similar public works |
| Natural resource projects, including flood control | Protecting land, water and natural areas; flood control |
| Local bonds and debt payments for these uses | Paying off borrowing for the above, and existing debt |
| Retirement benefits for government employees | Pensions and retiree obligations |
| Running the government: "the operations and administration of … municipalities, and the expenditures approved by" the governing body, "except those expenditures prohibited by general law" | Running the city or county itself, and possibly anything the commission approves unless a state law forbids it |

**Say this about the last row, plainly:** "Read literally, this last item may allow almost any expense the commission approves, until the Legislature passes a law saying otherwise. If your attorney reads it that broadly, very few programs will be affected and this screen becomes mostly a priorities exercise. If your attorney reads it narrowly, matching the 'core public needs' description voters saw on the ballot, more programs will need another source of money. It's worth deciding which reading you're planning around before we go program by program." Record the answer and use it throughout. If the user is unsure, run the screen on the narrower reading and note which programs would be safe under the broader one.

## Step 2: Build the program list

Use the government's own program list if it has one (program budgets, a service prioritization scoring, an "alternative budget" cut list). Otherwise build it from the budget by department and division. For each program, record:
- what it does, in one sentence;
- its cost, and how much of that comes from property tax (versus fees, grants, enterprise funds);
- the number of staff;
- any fees it already charges, and what share of its cost they cover.

Ask the user to correct the list and to add anything missing. Ask which programs residents care about most and which ones the governing body has said it wants to protect. **Priorities come from the user, not from you.**

## Step 3: First pass: sort the programs

Put each program in one of three groups and give a one-line reason:

- **Fits:** clearly within a listed use (for example, fire suppression, police patrol, road resurfacing, stormwater maintenance, pension contributions).
- **Unclear:** could fit depending on how it's described or run (for example, a library as education; a nature park as a natural resource project; parks capital work that includes drainage or flood storage; code enforcement as public safety).
- **Seems outside:** hard to place under any listed use except a broad reading of "operations and expenditures approved" (for example, recreation programs, special events, a performing arts center, aquatic centers, cultural grants).

Show the sorted list as a table. **Ask the user to confirm or move each program in the "unclear" and "seems outside" groups.** Their decision is final.

## Step 4: For each program they agree is outside, offer options

For every program the user places outside the permitted uses, show four options in plain words and let them choose (they may combine options):

**A. Redesign it so it fits a permitted use.** Offer concrete, honest ideas, and say what would change for the public. Examples (adapt to the real program):
- *Library:* focus property tax money on the educational core (early literacy, homework help, adult education, partnership with the school district), and fund the rest some other way. `[COUNSEL DETERMINATION]` whether that is enough to count as "education."
- *Nature park:* run and fund it as a natural resource / conservation project (habitat, water quality, land management), with programs and rentals funded by fees.
- *City park with drainage or flood storage:* pay for the stormwater and flood-control parts as infrastructure, and the rest from other sources.
- *Recreation center:* separate the public-safety or emergency-shelter function (if it's a designated shelter) from programming.

Never suggest relabeling a program without actually changing what it does. If the redesign is only a new name, say that it probably won't hold up and recommend another option.

**B. Pay for it another way.** Point to the tools in `revenue-options.md` that could fit this program: user fees, rentals, sponsorships, partners, grants, a nonprofit or private operator, an interlocal agreement. Give a rough sense of how much each could cover, with sources. Remind the user that most programs in this group can't be paid for by a special assessment (*Collier County*, *Lake County*); it is usually a fee or a partner. `[COUNSEL DETERMINATION]`

**C. Shrink it.** Fewer hours, fewer sites, a smaller program. Say what the public would notice.

**D. Stop it.** Say what goes away and what it saves.

For each choice, record: the option chosen, the money needed from the new source, and any follow-up question for counsel.

## Step 5: Turn the decisions into the revenue plan

Summarize the result in a plain table:

| Program | Decision | Paid for by | Amount needed | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Then go to the revenue plan (SKILL.md Phase 4). It now starts from this table: the money needed for programs moving off property tax, plus the overall shortfall from the exemption. The fees and assessments considered should follow from these decisions, not from a generic list.

## Things to watch

- **Keep the books able to show it.** The limit applies to property tax dollars. A program that moves off property tax still needs its full cost covered from the new source, or the property tax that's left has to be shown going only to permitted uses. Budget and accounting staff will need to show this. Flag it for the finance director.
- **Fees must stay tied to cost.** A fee can't be set higher than the program reasonably costs just to raise money (*Dunedin*). If fees can't cover a program, that's an answer too: the program needs a partner, a smaller footprint, or to stop.
- **Don't double count.** A program shrunk under C still needs its remaining cost assigned to a source.
- **Revisit after the Legislature acts.** A general law could narrow item (g) at any time. Note the date of the screen.
