I assumed you mean the City of Largo, since that's your folder's name, and the city's own parcels in your tax roll file confirm it. Largo expects to lose about $15.7 million a year once Amendment 3 is fully in effect. That's roughly a quarter of the property tax money that pays for Largo's services. The good news is that the amendment's new limits on what property tax can pay for probably don't force any program off property tax in Largo. The real problem is the size of the hole.

One note before anything else. In Florida, a chat like this about city business is probably a public record. Keep it, and don't put confidential legal strategy in it. If you're on the commission, don't share or discuss it with other members outside a noticed public meeting. Also, whether any fee or assessment below is legal is a question for a Florida-licensed attorney (likely your City Attorney) before anything is adopted.

My number is 14–17% below the city's. That's normal: I used this year's values with no growth, and I didn't include the redevelopment money. I'd plan around the city's figure.

What it means for residents:

  • About 12,100 of 15,800 homesteads (77%) would pay no city property tax at all.
  • Businesses and rentals would carry more of what's left. Their share of the city's taxable value rises from about 32% to about 39%.
  • The city could raise its tax rate to about 6.81 mills to make up the loss. That's under the state's 10-mill limit, but the increase would fall only on non-homesteads and on homes assessed above $250,000.

Saving programs: what I found so far

The limits on property tax use may not bite in Largo. Amendment 3 limits what cities can spend property tax on, and parks, the library and the Performing Arts Center are the questionable ones. But police (about $38.8M) and fire (about $30.8M) together already cost more than all of Largo's property tax (about $44M). So property tax could be shown going entirely to police and fire, with the library and parks paid from the city's other revenue. Whether the books can be kept that way is a call for your attorney.

These are the main ways to close the gap. Which ones to use is your choice, and they can be combined:

  1. A fire assessment. This is a yearly charge on each property for fire service. Homesteads pay it too, because the exemption doesn't cover it.
    • To show the scale, I tried made-up rates of $180 per home and 15¢ per square foot for businesses. That raises about $10M.
    • A typical homestead under $250,000 would still come out about $300 a year ahead. A rental home pays $180 more, and a 56,000 sq ft commercial building about $8,400 more.
    • Real rates need a study. The ambulance and medical (EMS) part of the fire budget can't be charged this way.
  2. Asking Pinellas County to fill its share. About $5.9M of the loss comes through county taxes. That covers the EMS money behind the two advanced life support (ambulance-level) units on the city's cut list. A letter to the county is part of this process.
  3. Fees and cost recovery. Examples are pool and tennis fees, Performing Arts Center rentals and ticket fees, and charging the city's utility funds for staff time they use. The mayor has said fees alone won't close the gap.
  4. A higher tax rate on the property that's still taxed, as above.

The deadline

To put any new assessment on the November 2027 tax bill, the commission has to pass a "resolution of intent" at a public hearing before January 1. That hearing needs four weekly newspaper notices first, so for a December hearing the first notice has to run in about two weeks. The deadline can move to March 1 if the Property Appraiser and Tax Collector agree. Passing the resolution keeps the option open but doesn't commit the city to charging anything. So, how much of the City programs could be saved by adopting assessments rather than taxes?

Claude, using the skill 126 min

An assessment could save somewhere between about half and nearly all of the programs on the City's cut list. It can't save the part of the loss that comes through county taxes. How much it saves depends on one number nobody has yet: how much of Largo Fire Rescue's cost is truly fire service, as opposed to ambulance and medical service.

What an assessment can pay for directly. In Florida, a special assessment can only pay for a service that benefits property: fire service (not ambulance or medical), stormwater, garbage, street lighting and local construction projects. Courts have ruled out using assessments for police, ambulance service, libraries, parks and recreation. So no assessment could pay for the library or the Performing Arts Center itself.

How it saves programs indirectly. Every dollar of fire cost moved onto an assessment frees a dollar of property tax. That freed money can cover police, and the City's other revenue can then cover parks, the library and the Center. In practice, each assessment dollar saves about a dollar of programs somewhere in the budget.

The limit. Largo Fire Rescue cost about $30.8M in FY 2025-26, but about 90% of its calls in 2022 were medical. Ambulance and medical costs can't be put on an assessment, and the County already pays part of the department's cost under the EMS agreement. A careful study might support $10M of true fire cost, or it might support less. That study (draft 05) is what sets the ceiling, so the honest answer today is "between about $5M and $10M, depending on the study."

What an assessment can't fix. The other $5.9M of the $15.7M loss comes through county taxes, mostly for EMS. The City can't replace that with an assessment, so it depends on the County (draft 04). Without County help, the two ambulance-level units on the cut list are at risk whatever Largo does with an assessment.

Smaller extras. If the City already charges for stormwater or garbage, raising those charges to cover the full cost of each service may free up a little more General Fund money. That would likely add hundreds of thousands of dollars, not millions. The City's fee records will show how much.

So with the County's share set aside, a fire assessment could plausibly replace somewhere between half and nearly all of the City's own loss. Even at the high end, a typical homestead under $250,000 still comes out about $300 a year ahead.

is it possible to create a special assessment for road maintenance?

Claude, using the skill 127 min

Yes, but only in a narrow form. It would also do much less for Largo than a fire assessment.

What has a solid footing: street-by-street projects. State law lets a city charge the properties along a street for paving, repaving, repairing or widening that street. That's chapter 170 of the Florida Statutes, and the City's home-rule power covers similar projects. The charge is usually split by how much street frontage each lot has. Courts have upheld these when the work clearly benefits the properties being charged (City of Boca Raton v. State, 1992). The catch is that each one is a separate project with its own notices and hearing. It pays for construction work, not the yearly cost of a road crew, and charging a neighborhood for its own repaving tends to be unpopular.

What's risky: a citywide charge for general road upkeep. The Florida Supreme Court struck down Port Orange's "transportation utility fee," which charged developed property to pay for road maintenance, and treated it as a tax (State v. City of Port Orange, 1994). That case was about a fee, not an assessment. But the same problem would face a citywide assessment: Largo's road network serves everyone who drives on it, so it's hard to show that any particular property benefits in a special way. With an attorney who wants to avoid risk, I'd expect this to be ruled out. That's a call for the City Attorney.

Why it wouldn't save much in Largo:

  • Road spending from the General Fund is small. It was about $1.74M in FY 2022-23 (Florida League of Cities data packet), compared with fire's tens of millions. Much of Largo's road work is likely paid from the gas tax and the Penny for Pinellas sales tax already, so moving it to an assessment wouldn't free property tax. The budget detail in the records request would confirm this.
  • Roads don't have a spending-limit problem. Roads are on Amendment 3's list of things property tax may still pay for, so they don't need to come off property tax.
  • Fire is the bigger lever. Moving road costs onto an assessment would free property tax dollar for dollar, the same way a fire assessment does. Fire is simply the much larger budget item.

Where road-type assessments can help: if the City has a specific capital project it now plans to pay for from the General Fund, such as resurfacing a particular neighborhood, sidewalks, or putting power lines underground. Paying for that one project with a chapter 170 assessment frees that much General Fund money for other programs.

If the budget detail turns up road costs in the General Fund, I can add a chapter 170 project assessment to the draft set.
